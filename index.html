A Walk Along the Critical Line

An illustrated tour of the first computer-checked proof of the Riemann Hypothesis

Each page holds one idea. If you've met calculus and complex numbers, you have everything you need.

Some pages are marked real data, some schematic, and a few our reading, where the picture is an interpretation and not something the proof says.

Wherever a picture says so, it is computed live in your browser from real primes and real zeros of ζ.

Some numbers can't be split into smaller factors: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, … These are the primes.

Cross out the multiples of 2, then of 3, then of 5 and 7. Whatever survives is prime, and every other number is built by multiplying them.

Up close, the primes look scattered at random.

Sometimes two arrive side by side. Sometimes there's a long silence. No simple rule tells you where the next one will land.

But step back, and a pattern appears.

Walk up the number line and, at each prime p, step up by ln p. (Prime powers like 4, 8 and 9 add a step too, the same size as their prime's.) Call the running total ψ(x). It climbs almost exactly along the line y = x.

real data

How far does the staircase stray from the line? Subtract the line and plot only what's left over, ψ(x) − x. It wobbles back and forth.

The Riemann Hypothesis says the wobbles stay about the size of √x, forever.

real data, primes up to 200,000

Why √x? Flip a fair coin n times and count heads minus tails. The count drifts, but only by about √n.

So the Riemann Hypothesis says the primes are as fair as coins: no hidden bias, just honest noise.

In 1859 Bernhard Riemann studied one function:

ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + 1/4s + ⋯

Euler had noticed it secretly knows the primes: the same number is also a product with one factor per prime. Watch the sum and the product race to the same value.

To go further, Riemann needed numbers that live on a flat plane instead of along a line.

Each of these complex numbers is an arrow from the centre. Its real part says how far right it reaches, and its height (the imaginary part, marked with an i) says how far up. To add two of them, lay the arrows head to tail.

Now feed ζ one of these numbers, s = σ + i·t. Each term 1/nˢ becomes an arrow: σ sets its length, 1/nσ, and the height t turns it by an angle t · ln n.

So ζ(s) is a chain of arrows laid head to tail, and its value is where the chain ends. Watch the chain curl as t rises.

computed live

Now set the real part to exactly ½ and raise the height. At t = 14.1347… the chain ends exactly where it started. The arrows cancel perfectly, so ζ(s) = 0.

That point is a zero of ζ.

One honest wrinkle: at real part ½ the plain sum never settles, so this picture uses its cousin with alternating signs, 1 − 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ − ⋯. It does settle, and it has exactly the same zeros here.

computed live, 600 arrows

Do this at every point s of the plane, and record how far from the start each chain ends: |ζ(s)|. That gives a landscape: light where the chain ends far away, dark where it ends close to home.

The ground drops all the way to zero only at the zeros. The lowest is the 14.13 you just watched. They are the heart of the story.

Left of real part 0 no sum settles at all; there ζ is extended smoothly from the rest of the map. No zeros in this picture lie out there.

computed live

Every zero ever found sits on one vertical line, where the real part is exactly ½. It's called the critical line.

The Riemann Hypothesis says they all do. Computers have checked more than ten trillion, but there are infinitely many, so checking can never finish.

the first 200 zeros

Here's why zeros matter. Each zero is a pure tone, and the primes are the music.

Start from the smooth line y = x and add one wave for each zero. With more and more zeros, the staircase ψ(x) from earlier appears, with a step at every prime, right where the primes are.

the first 200 zeros

A zero on the line makes a wave whose size grows like √x.

A zero off the line, with real part β bigger than ½, would make a wave growing like xβ. Sooner or later it drowns out everything else. One off-key note would spoil the whole song.

schematic

There isn't just one ζ. Color the primes with a repeating pattern of values, such as +1 and −1, called a Dirichlet character, and you get a new zeta function with its own zeros.

The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis says all of those zeros sit on the line too. This proof covers ζ and every one of these new functions.

In November 2026 the mathematician Levent Alpöge, working with Claude, released a proof. Not a paper: 175,187 files of Lean, a language in which a computer checks every logical step.

No human has read it all yet. So we read it like a map.

The proof has six steps. We'll walk them backwards from the goal, because each step answers the question the one before it leaves open.

First A, then B, then D, then C. Steps E and F supply background facts that the main chain rests on. Both hold with plenty of room to spare, so we visit them briefly near the end.

The proof watches one quantity, G(x): a smoothed version of the prime total, minus its expected trend.

If RH is true, G(x) divided by √x stays inside a thin band forever. Here it is, computed from every prime up to 200,000.

real data

Now imagine one zero just off the line. Its wave grows, and the crests climb higher and higher.

So to rule such a zero out, it's enough to show G(x) never gets too high. Not a two-sided band: just a ceiling.

hypothetical zero added to real data

Stand at one point z and record a list of numbers about a function there: its value, its slope, how it curves, and so on. These are its Taylor coefficients.

Add up c₀ + c₁(s − z) + c₂(s − z)² + ⋯ term by term and the function comes back, like a sketch gaining detail. But only out to a limit: past the nearest point where the function blows up, a pole, the sum falls apart however many terms you add.

That limit leaves a fingerprint in the list itself. If the nearest pole is a distance r away, the coefficients grow like (1/r)k. The closer the trouble, the faster they grow.

So the list works like a storm warning, and that is the signal Landau's trap listens for. (ζ has a pole too, at s = 1.)

In 1905 Edmund Landau found a way to turn a ceiling into a trap. Repackage G as a new function (its Laplace transform) whose poles sit exactly at the zeros of ζ. A ceiling of size xθ draws a line at real part θ.

A zero right of that line is a pole close to the point z, so the Taylor coefficients at z must grow fast (red). But the ceiling keeps them small (green). Both can't be true, so no such zero exists.

schematic

The proof can't check the ceiling everywhere. Its machinery is built for one stretch of x at a time, so it proves the ceiling only on scattered stretches called windows. Each runs from some number Y up to Y1.5, and there is always another one further out.

Is that enough? Think of a car that speeds the whole way. You don't need a camera on every mile: one camera far enough down the road will catch it. A rogue zero is like that car. Its trouble shows up at every scale far enough out, so one window far enough out catches it.

So what plays the camera? Landau's trap never reads G all at once: it works through the Taylor coefficients, and each coefficient only looks at one stretch of x. The gold bump shows the stretch it sees.

Further out, a coefficient's view fits almost entirely inside one window, and the fit keeps improving. There the ceiling controls its reading, and that is where a rogue zero gets caught.

So everything comes down to one sentence:

show that G(x) is at most about √x, on enough windows.

All the exotic machinery from here on serves that one goal.

The proof's machine has a blind spot. It's built from two helper primes, and they give every other prime two labels, each +1 or −1. That sorts the primes into four bins, and the machine can only count one of them.

Why only one? The machine works inside a bigger number system built from the helpers. There, many primes break apart, the way 5 = (2 + i)(2 − i) once you allow i, while 3 stays whole. The machine can only weigh primes that stay whole: those are the χ = −1 primes. An averaging step then narrows them to ψ = +1.

toy helpers 3 and 7our reading of the source

To get all the primes back, the proof uses the simplest case of Fourier analysis: splitting something into pure patterns, the way an equalizer splits music into bass and treble. Here the patterns are four rows of ±1 signs.

Add the rows as shown. Every prime outside the gold bin cancels to 0, and every prime inside counts 4 times. So one bin = ¼ × (all primes + three helper sums).

The helpers are hand-picked: most possible helpers are known to behave well, so the proof chooses good ones, whose sums are small. A ceiling on one bin becomes a ceiling on all primes.

How does the machine bound one bin? With a trick from 1850: measure one number in two ways.

Take a! = 1 · 2 · 3 ⋯ a. Measure it by its primes, or measure it by its size. The two bars must be the same length, so the size teaches you about the primes.

How many times does the prime 3 divide 81!? Every third number gives one factor. Every ninth gives a second. Every 27th, a third.

That's Legendre's formula: about a/p + a/p² + a/p³ + ⋯ factors of p.

The proof adds a twist. It measures a huge number a against a smaller one b, with a = x · b.

At each prime power pᵉ it keeps only the overflow: how far a/pᵉ sticks out above b. Once pᵉ passes x, nothing sticks out.

Add up the overflows, each weighted by ln p, and out comes exactly the prime sum inside G(x).

Subtract the size side, and what's left is b · G(x). The quantity Step A needs is the gap between two ways of measuring.

Where does the proof make its two-way count? Inside a classical machine called a theta lift. Think of it as a gearbox that carries a perfectly symmetric object from one world into a partner world.

For us, one feature matters. It meshes two polynomials, one of huge degree a and one of small degree b, with a = x · b, like a big gear driving a small one. (A polynomial's degree is its highest power: x³ + 1 has degree 3.)

schematic

Picture the polynomials being passed around a round table. Each one has an ordinary part and a mirror-image part (think of z and its mirror image z̄). Each seat has a big pocket, with room for degree a, and a small pocket, with room for degree b.

At the home seat, the ordinary part goes in the big pocket. At every other seat around the table, the two pockets are swapped.

schematic

At a prime p there's a magic move. In arithmetic mod p, (u + v)p = up + vp, because every middle coefficient is divisible by p.

So the substitution x → xp, called Frobenius, plays nicely with everything. It divides a polynomial's degree by p, and it passes the polynomial to the next seat.

Follow the big degree around the table. After e hops it's a/pᵉ.

Even hops land on a seat where it goes in the big pocket: it fits. Odd hops land on a swapped seat, where it must squeeze into the small pocket of size b. Whatever sticks out, shown in red, turns into forced factors of p.

Try other values of x and p.

That one picture explains the shape of the overflow formula from Step D.

Dividing by pᵉ is Frobenius. Subtracting b is the small pocket. Counting only odd e is the hops that land on swapped seats. And once pᵉ is past x, everything fits.

our reading of the source's formulas

Forced factors are good news. The polynomials form a lattice, a grid of points. If every one of them is divisible by p, you may divide the whole lattice by p.

The grid gets denser, and in this kind of geometry a denser lattice counts as bigger. The proof builds the lattice it measures in exactly this way.

The size side is measured "at infinity", with the areas of very special lattices in the complex plane.

A formula of Chowla and Selberg computes those areas exactly, and Euler's constant γ falls out: the same γ that G(x) subtracts.

Geometry then says the books can't be too unbalanced: the prime side is at most the size side, plus errors. For one lift that reads

b · G(x) ≤ errors

But the errors are about b · x, far too big to say anything. One lift isn't enough.

So do it again. Feed the output of one lift into another, m times. Each lift turns one minus sign of its group into a plus.

Every round forces its own factors of p, and the gains add up.

The price: every lift adds a block of new coordinates, so lift number j has more to carry than the ones before. It costs about j.

The whole chain costs about 1 + 2 + ⋯ + m ≈ m²/2.

matches Rubinstein's independent account

Put it together. In units of b:

m · G(x) ≤ x + m²

The gap G, collected once per lift, is at most an overhead x paid once, plus the chain's cost. Divide by m:

G(x) ≤ x/m + m

A longer chain spreads the overhead over more rounds, but costs more. Where's the sweet spot?

Set the derivative to zero: m = √x, and then G(x) ≤ 2√x. That's exactly the size the Riemann Hypothesis needs.

Drag the red point.

It could easily have gone wrong. If each lift had to redo the bookkeeping of every lift before it, the chain would cost m³, and the best bound would be x2/3: useless.

The proof charges every piece of bookkeeping to exactly one lift.

Why does the author call each lift "one more order of multiplicity"? The phrase comes from a cousin of RH for clock arithmetic (like mod 7), proved by André Weil in the 1940s.

In 1969 Sergei Stepanov found a short proof: build a polynomial with a double, triple, … root (multiplicity m) at every point you want to count. Degree D allows at most D roots counted this way, so (points) × m ≤ D.

This proof has the same shape: G × m ≤ x + m². Multiplicity normally needs derivatives, which whole numbers lack. The chain of lifts stands in for them.

the comparison is our reading

Step D's count is averaged over a huge family of pieces, one for each of an enormous number of code words. That average is only fair if the pieces barely overlap, like a room full of microphones where each one hears only its own instrument.

In very high dimensions, random directions are almost always nearly perpendicular. The proof shows its pieces overlap by at most about e−xε/8, far less than it needs. The tool is a classical identity called the Siegel–Weil formula.

random directions, computed live

The proof works inside gigantic number systems, of degree around x². They carry their own arithmetic noise, measured by objects called Ш ("Sha") and class groups, which could distort the lattices being measured.

The noise only has to stay below about exε/2, a very generous limit, and deep classical theorems keep it far below. Like Step E, it has room to spare: the ½ is decided by Step C alone.

schematic

Now run the whole story forward.

A chain of √x lifts bounds one bin of primes. Fourier analysis recovers all the primes. That puts a ceiling on G(x), on windows. And Landau's trap leaves no room for a zero right of ½.

A small trusted core of Lean, the kernel, checked every step. Only Lean's standard axioms are used, and the statement is the community library's own.

By 22 November, independent rebuilds matched byte for byte, five independently written checkers had accepted the proof, and the Lean and Mathlib maintainers had certified both statement and proof. One official end-to-end run was still finishing. Checked is not the same as understood: no one has yet written out the core argument by hand.

status as of 22 Nov 2026

Why did this take 160 years? Part of the answer is a wall that has stood since 1969. In clock arithmetic, Stepanov's proof works because polynomials there have a variable to differentiate. Over the whole numbers there is none: what is the derivative of 12?

The breakthrough is a way over the wall. A chain of theta lifts has the same effect as a higher-order zero, with no derivative at all. Charging each lift only for its own bookkeeping then lands the bound exactly on √x.

the author's claim, plus our reading

The author's timeline runs from 6 October to 16 November 2026: about six weeks. Three things made that possible.

The route runs through six specialties that rarely meet, and an AI that has read all of them can hold them at once. Lean makes trying cheap: a bad idea fails in minutes, and a good one arrives already checked. And most of the 175,187 files rebuild a century of known theorems that the new idea stands on.

our reading

A great many published results begin "assuming GRH". If the proof holds, they become theorems. For example, Miller's fast primality test becomes provably correct, primes in every progression arrive with near-perfect regularity, and the dreaded "Siegel zeros" cannot exist.

The author previews more: RH for all number fields "with more time", a new proof of the Weil and Deligne bounds, and consequences in K-theory. None of that is in the code yet.

previews are the author's, not checked

Some new mathematics is already visible in the files. To reach RH, the proof built whole libraries that had never been written in checkable form, plus a counting theorem that works over any number field.

Those are reusable whether or not anyone needs them for RH again. One caution: the proof restates 444 published theorems in its own words, and someone still needs to check that each restatement says what the original says.

What nobody has yet is a human understanding of why it works.

The pictures in this book are a first reading, not the last word. The author expects the proof to be "massively simplified, likely immediately."

The last few pages take some first steps toward a why.

On 21 November the mathematician Michael Rubinstein posted a 164-page working draft, written with three AI systems, that explains the proof for the zeta function in the style of classical number theory.

It reads the whole proof as the two-way count of Step D, run with a far better number, and reduces everything to one inequality, (M): divisibility against size, compared m times over. The road from (M) to RH is written out by hand. (M) itself is still taken from the Lean code.

from the draft's own summary

What is the better number? The draft starts with the lemniscate sine, the inverse of ∫ dt/√(1 − t⁴). Its derivatives at 0 are whole numbers: 1, −12, 3024, −4390848, …

A prime like 3, 7 or 11 (remainder 3 after dividing by 4) divides the n-th one about n·p/(p² − 1) times: a smooth formula, with none of the rounding in Legendre's. Primes like 5 and 13 are not forced to divide at all. That is the blind spot behind Step B's bins.

computed exactly, n up to 201

To see why an idea works, try it somewhere the answer is already known. Clock arithmetic is such a place.

Solve y² = x³ − x + 1 on a clock with p hours. The number of solutions always lands near p. In 1933 Helmut Hasse proved it is never off by more than 2√p, and Weil later proved a bound of the same kind for every curve. This is the Riemann Hypothesis of clock arithmetic.

counted live

Here is how far off the count is, for every prime clock size up to 3,000. It wobbles the way the primes did near the start of this book, and stays inside a square-root envelope.

On clocks this is a theorem with several proofs. Two of them explain the square root in two different ways, and each has a counterpart in the new proof.

counted live

Stepanov's proof builds a polynomial with a root of multiplicity m at every point being counted. Each extra order of vanishing adds a batch of new conditions, which cost about m² in total. The overhead, about p, is paid only once. With constants dropped:

(error) × m ≲ p + m²

That is the shape of this proof's inequality, and in both the best m is a square root.

schematic: constants droppedthe match is our reading

Hasse's theorem also has a short proof. Counting the points involves a map called Frobenius, and maps between curves have a degree: a whole number that is never negative.

For whole numbers r and s, the degree of "r minus s copies of Frobenius" is r² + a·r·s + p·s², where a is how far the count is off (+9 on the first page of this part). A quadratic that never dips below zero has discriminant at most 0. So a² ≤ 4p, which says |a| ≤ 2√p.

the textbook proof, exact

So the square root has two classic explanations: a balance, and a positivity. The new proof visibly uses the balance.

Weil showed in 1952 that RH for the whole numbers is equivalent to a certain quantity never being negative. This proof only ever uses one-sided bounds: a ceiling for Landau's trap, an upper bound on lattice sizes. Perhaps its two-way count is a positivity in disguise. Whoever can name it may have found the "why".

speculation

Nothing in m·G ≤ x + m² forces m = √x. A chain of length xα gives G ≤ x1−α + xα, and Step A, as written in the code, turns any bound xθ into "no zeros with real part above θ".

So a short chain should give a weaker result. Even α = 0.01 would rule out zeros above 0.99, which nobody had proved before. If so, a short chain is the smallest case worth understanding. The code itself only uses chains near √x.

A computer-checked proof can be taken apart by experiment: change one choice, rebuild, and see which step breaks. Whatever survives every change is the core.
