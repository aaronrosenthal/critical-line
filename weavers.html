The Weavers and the Middle Wire

A story about the first computer-checked proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.

Press → or tap the arrow to turn the page

Kito hatched in a colony of weaver birds, the kind that tie real knots. Every evening the whole colony settled on three long wires to sing, and every evening somebody asked the old question: will a bird ever perch above the middle wire?

Old Grey, the oldest weaver, always said the same thing. “Ten trillion birds, and not one has. But watching isn't knowing.” In the old tree hung the torn nests of every weaver who had tried to settle it. Kito decided to find out.

Grey began with twigs. “Every bundle in this colony is tied from twigs that can't be split: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11… Twelve is a bundle of 2, 2 and 3, and there's no other way to tie it.”

Kito found them scattered with no pattern at all: 41 and 43 side by side, then nothing from 89 to 97.

At dusk the colony counted its twigs aloud against a smooth guess. The two never quite agreed, and the difference rose and fell like a murmur: the colony's chorus. “How loud can it get?” asked Kito. “That,” said Grey, “is the whole question.”

In the mathThe twigs are the primes; every whole number factors into them one way. The chorus: prime powers up to x, each weighted by log p, minus x. RH says it stays about √x.

Grey kept an old songbook, written down by Bernhard Riemann in 1859. Every song in it is built from the twigs. “Race it two ways,” said Grey.

One bird added note after note: 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + ⋯. The other used nothing but twigs, one factor for each, multiplied together. Both stopped at exactly the same mark, π²/6.

“So the songbook knows the twigs,” said Grey, “and it can tell us about the chorus.”

In the mathζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + ⋯ equals a product with one factor per prime (Euler). The zeros of ζ are the birds of this story.

Then Grey showed Kito where the chorus comes from. Hidden on the wires sits a flock of birds, and each one sings a single pure note.

Add their voices and the jagged twig count appears, step by step. With a hundred birds singing it is already close.

In the mathRiemann's explicit formula, proved by von Mangoldt: the prime-count error is a sum of waves, one for each zero of ζ. A zero's position along the line sets its pitch.

The birds perch on three wires. None ever sits outside the outer two, and they come in mirror pairs: a bird above the middle wire would have a twin just as far below.

“Every bird anyone has spotted sits on the middle wire,” said Grey. “More than ten trillion. But the flock never ends.”

In the mathThe zeros lie in the strip 0 < Re s < 1, symmetric about Re s = ½. The Riemann Hypothesis: every one has Re s = ½. Computers have checked more than 1013.

birds at the first 13 zeros

That night Kito couldn't sleep, so Kito flew. The path swung out in long loops and curled back, again and again, while Grey watched from below.

Every so often the flight swept exactly over the home perch. Each time, a bird fluttered down and settled on the middle wire, at the very hour Kito passed overhead. By the end of the night, six birds sat in a row.

In the mathKito's position at hour t is ζ(½ + i·t), and the home perch is ζ = 0. So each pass over it is a zero, ½ + i·t: the first at t = 14.13. These are the birds of the story.

“Why does it matter where they sit?” asked Kito. Grey hopped up the pole.

“On the middle wire a bird sings at the chorus's normal volume, about √x. Sit higher, and its voice grows faster than everyone else's. Sooner or later it drowns them all out.”

In the mathA zero at height β contributes a wave of size xβ. On the middle line that is x½ = √x.

Drag the bird up and down.

Kito wasn't the only one waiting. Across the valley lay other colonies. Each paints its twigs with its own repeating pattern of +1 and −1, and each has its own songbook and its own flock on its own three wires.

Weavers in every colony had built on a hope. On a high shelf sat their eggs, each labelled “assuming GRH”: assuming no bird, in any colony, ever perches above the middle wire. Not one could hatch until someone proved it.

“Settle it for us alone,” said Grey, “and the eggs stay cold. A real answer must hold for every colony at once.”

In the mathEach pattern is a Dirichlet character with its own L-function. The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) says all their zeros sit on the middle line. Many published results assume it. This proof covers ζ and every one of them.

One November morning the colony woke to find a new nest in the old tree, among the torn ones. It was finished: 175,187 knots, woven by a mathematician named Levent Alpöge and a mysterious helper called Claude: not a bird at all, but a new kind of mind, made of words.

Before anyone dared touch it, the knot inspector tugged every knot. Not one slipped, and it was built to hold for every colony across the valley. But it was far too big to take in at once. “We won't understand it by staring,” said Grey. “We'll read it like a map.”

In the mathThe proof is 175,187 files of Lean, a language in which a small trusted program, the kernel, checks every logical step. Claude is an AI model made by Anthropic.

The map showed six landmarks. “Walk it backwards,” said Grey, “from where we want to end up. And weave a practice nest as we go. That's how a weaver learns one.”

First, a trap for birds nobody can see. Then four piles of twigs. Then an old balance. Then the great loom. Two more landmarks lay off the road, toward the Far Ones' country.

In the mathSix steps. The proof's own notes label them A, B, D, C, E, F, because it was written in a different order than you walk it. Each step answers the question the one before it leaves open.

To hear the chorus clearly, Grey taught Kito a listener's trick. The raw chorus is a clatter. Each twig claps once, sharply, the moment it arrives, and every clap jolts the chorus.

“So listen for the hum,” said Grey. Now each twig starts humming the moment it arrives, at nothing, and swells steadily night after night. Nothing jumps any more: same twigs, same birds, only easier to hear. The colony calls this smoothed chorus G.

If every bird sits on the middle wire, G stays inside a thin band, about √x wide, forever. Kito listened all the way out to 200,000. It stayed inside.

In the mathRaw: ψ(x) − x, which jumps by ln p at each prime power. Smoothed: G(x) = Σ Λ(n)(x/n − 1) minus its trend; twig n's term starts at 0 when x = n and grows linearly. The same zeros decide G. Plotted: G(x)/√x.

real data

“First, a trap,” said Grey, “for birds you can't see. We can't search an endless wire bird by bird. But a bird perched above the middle wire sings too loud, so we can listen instead.

“If the chorus never rises above about √x, even on one side only, no bird can hide above the middle wire, and so its mirror twin can't hide below.”

“But how can listening catch anything?” asked Kito. “I'll show you,” said Grey.

In the mathOne-sided bounds are enough: Edmund Landau, 1905.

Grey led Kito to the first landmark, a deep shaft with the wires strung across it. “Call down,” said Grey. The nearer a bird in the shaft, the faster its echoes grow.

So nearness is height: a bird above the middle wire is nearer to Kito than any bird on it. But a chorus never louder than about √x allows echoes only at the middle wire's pace. A bird up there would crack that limit. So no bird sits up there.

“That's all?” said Kito. “That's all,” said Grey, “as long as we can prove the ceiling.”

In the mathThe echoes are the Taylor coefficients of a function built from the chorus, taken at a point right of the wires. A singularity at distance d makes them grow like (1/d)k. The picture draws distance as height; in the proof, positivity does that (Landau, 1905).

Drag the bird down to the middle wire.

schematic

There was a catch. The weavers can listen only on certain stretches of nights, each much longer than the last. Kito's heart sank. “That's all we need,” said Grey gently. “A bird above the middle wire is too loud everywhere. Some dawn far enough out will catch it.”

But the trap still needed its ceiling: proof that on those dawns the chorus stays below about √x. “Who can promise that?” asked Kito. Grey looked down the road. “Only the great loom. And the road there runs through the twig piles.”

That night Kito knotted the first strands of a practice nest. At first light they set off.

In the mathWindows from Y to Y3/2, reaching to infinity, suffice. The bound needed is x½+ε. To get it, the proof ties a bundle, weighs it two ways, and weaves a lattice to settle the difference.

The road ran straight into the second landmark: four big piles of twigs, and the first wall. The loom ahead is strung from two helper twigs that sort every twig into these four piles, and it can weigh only one: the gold pile.

“Why only one?” asked Kito. “Inside the loom,” said Grey, “most twigs split into pieces. Only whole ones can be weighed.”

Kito stared at the other three piles. “Then we're blind to three twigs out of four.”

In the mathThe helpers give each prime two labels, χ, ψ = ±1. Inside the bigger number system some primes split, like 5 = (2 + i)(2 − i), while 3 stays whole. The loom sees the χ = −1 half; averaging narrows it to ψ = +1.

“Not blind,” said Grey. “Just looking from the wrong side. Write the four piles as four rows of signs, and add the rows.”

Kito did. Every twig outside the gold pile cancelled to nothing; every twig inside counted four times. “So a limit on one pile,” said Kito slowly, “is a limit on all of them?” “If the helpers are quiet,” said Grey. “And we get to choose quiet ones.” Kito wove the standing ring that evening.

In the mathOne pile = ¼ × (all primes + three helper sums): Fourier analysis on four classes. Known averages guarantee good helpers exist.

this exact combination is in the Lean code

How do you hold a chorus down? Grey's plan was to weigh it, and for that the colony needed help.

Past the edge of the colony live the Far Ones, “at infinity”, in directions no bird can point to. From the edge, Kito saw only a shadow: a cage of light that kept turning itself inside out.

The Far Ones can't count twigs. But they can measure size, and carry strands. They follow fixed rules, and the inspector checks them too.

In the mathTwigs stand for the primes. The Far Ones stand for the place “at infinity” (size, in the real and complex numbers) and the high-dimensional spaces the proof works in.

our framing

At the third landmark stood an old balance, older than anyone in the colony. “Tie the biggest bundle you can,” said Grey. “Weigh it by its twigs on this side, and let a Far One weigh it by its size on that one.”

The scale settled, perfectly level. Of course it did: a bundle is exactly as long as its twigs laid end to end.

“A level scale teaches nothing,” said Grey. “But we're about to tip it.”

In the mathThe bundle is a! = 1 · 2 · 3 ⋯ a, and Σ (copies of p in a!) · log p = log(a!). Chebyshev, 1850.

“Before we tip it,” said Grey, “how many 3-twigs are in the big bundle?”

Kito counted in layers. Every third number gives one. Every ninth gives a second. Every 27th, a third, and the last one more. Forty in all. “Layers,” said Grey. “Remember layers.”

In the math81! contains 27 + 9 + 3 + 1 = 40 threes. Legendre's formula: about a/p + a/p² + a/p³ + ⋯ copies of p.

Then Grey tipped the scale on purpose. From each layer's pile Grey took away one fixed basketful, b, and weighed only what spilled over.

Small twigs turn up everywhere: a 2 in every 2nd bundle, but an 11 only in every 11th. So the small twigs' piles spill far over the rim, while rarer twigs and higher layers fit inside.

In the mathWith a = x · b: each basket holds b, and layer pe gives about a/pe copies. Keep (a/pe − b)+; past pe = x it is zero.

Drag x.

Kito weighed the spill-over, each twig by its length. On the other pan, a Far One weighed the same thing by size alone.

The twig pan is the real count, twig by twig. The size pan is the smooth guess. So the scale tipped, just slightly, and the tilt was exactly their difference: the chorus.

“To hold the chorus down,” said Grey, “we must stop this scale from tipping too far.” That night the practice nest got its roof.

In the mathΣ log p · (a/pe − b)+, minus the size side, equals b · G(x). The trap needs a limit on this gap.

At last they reached the great loom. “The balance shows what to weigh,” said Grey. “The loom weighs it a second way, by weaving.”

Grey fed out a strand of enormous length, a, and a Far One carried it over into a partner world, where it was paired with a short one of length b, like thread passed from a big hoop to a small one.

In the mathA pass is a theta lift. The strands are polynomials of degree a and b, with a = x · b (a polynomial's degree is its highest power).

schematic

Kito watched a pass up close. The strand hopped along a row of pegs, and each peg held a big loop, with room for length a, and a small one, with room for only b.

At the home peg, the strand's gold side went in the big loop. On the way to the next peg a Far One turned it over, and it arrived with its gold side in the small loop. At the peg after that it was turned back again.

“What if it doesn't fit?” asked Kito. “Watch,” said Grey.

In the mathEach polynomial has a z part and a z̄ part. At every other peg the two swap roles.

schematicthe turn-over picture: our framing

At each hop, a strand shrinks by its twig. Every other hop it lands in a big loop and fits. In between it must squeeze into a small loop, and whatever sticks out, in red, the weave is forced to tie off as knots.

Kito stared. “Those are the overhangs from the baskets!” They were the same amounts, found a second way. Every overhang on the scale became a knot on the loom.

In the mathFrobenius, x → xp, divides degree by p, since (u + v)p = up + vp mod p. Odd hops overflow by a/pe − b: Step D's formula.

Grey hopped along the beam, tying the forced knots. “Knots pack a weave tighter,” said Grey, “and a weave can only be packed so tight.”

To the Far Ones, measuring at infinity, every knot made the mesh finer, and a finer mesh counts as bigger. There's only so much room. So there's only so much room for knots, and the knots are the overhangs, so only so much room for the chorus.

In the mathThe polynomials form a lattice. Divisibility at p shrinks its covolume, and this is how the proof builds the lattice it weighs.

the squeeze: our reading

There was still the Far One's pan to read exactly. The smooth guess has one awkward number in it: Euler's constant γ, 0.5772….

Two panes of light hung from the loom. A Far One measured the area of each, and out of the measurement fell γ, exactly. Now both pans could be read to the last digit.

In the mathThe Chowla–Selberg formula gives the periods of CM lattices exactly, and γ appears.

Kito worked out one pass and slumped down beside the scale. The squeeze was real, but loose: the room left over was about b · x, far bigger than the chorus itself.

“One pass,” said Grey, “tells you nothing.” Kito's first wall was the same: one layer thin, with daylight through it.

In the mathGeometry gives b · G(x) ≤ errors, but the errors are about b · x. Useless on its own.

“Then weave again,” said Grey. Feed one pass into the next, m times over.

Each new pass forces its own knots, so the chorus gets squeezed again, m times in all, while the big slack is paid only once. With each new layer, Kito turned one more bead on the sign cord from minus to plus.

In the mathA chain of m theta lifts. Each turns one minus sign of its group into a plus.

But weaving isn't free. Each new pass opens directions that only the Far Ones can carry, and each pass carries a little more than the one before.

Kito added it up and frowned. The cost grew much faster than the passes did.

In the mathPass j costs about j blocks of new coordinates, so m passes cost about 1 + 2 + ⋯ + m ≈ m²/2.

matches Rubinstein's independent accountthe ½ in m²/2: our arithmetic

Kito wrote it all out as a budget. After m passes the chorus has been counted m times. Against that: the slack x, paid once, and the weaving cost, about m².

So m times the chorus is at most x + m². Share the slack over the passes, and the chorus must fit in what's left: x/m + m.

In the mathIn units of b: m · G(x) ≤ x + m², so G(x) ≤ x/m + m. The write-ups call the smaller error lines “budgets” (B₀, Nc); each fits in the same allowance.

"budgets", "allowance": the write-ups' words

How many passes? Too few, and the slack crushes you. Too many, and the weaving cost does.

Kito walked down into the valley between, and at the very bottom, where Grey was already waiting, found the answer: the square root of x. There, the chorus fits exactly where the Riemann Hypothesis needs it.

Back home, Kito wove the walls √x layers deep.

In the mathx/m + m is smallest at m = √x, giving G ≤ 2√x.

Grey had seen it nearly fail. In an early count, each pass seemed to re-weave every pass before it, and the cost ran away.

“Then the best you get,” said Grey, “is far too loud, and the trap stays open.” The finished loom pays for each knot exactly once.

In the mathIf the chain cost m³, x/m + m² is smallest near m = x1/3, giving x2/3 instead of √x. Alpöge's timeline: an early count “would stop at 2/3”.

“I've seen this shape before,” said Grey. “Long ago a weaver named Stepanov tied every knot over and over, and with that one trick settled the question in a round aviary far from here.”

“Then why not here?” “To tie a knot twice you need a kind of slope, and whole numbers have none. That wall stood for more than fifty years, until these passes got over it.”

In the mathStepanov, 1969: a polynomial of degree D with roots of multiplicity m at the points gives points × m ≤ D. Here, G × m ≤ x + m². Multiplicity needs derivatives, and integers have none. The author calls each lift “one more order of multiplicity”.

the multiplicity claim is the author's; the comparison is ours

Off the main road stood the last two landmarks. Two smaller things had to hold for the squeeze to work.

At the Far Ones' gate, the loom's many strands had to stay untangled: in their many directions, almost any two run nearly at right angles. And deep in the giant number systems, the weather had to stay calm.

Neither decides the answer, but without them the loom would fail. Kito added the doorway last, woven tight against the weather.

Now Kito ran the whole story forward. The loom's √x passes squeezed the chorus on the gold pile. Grey's rows of signs brought back all four piles. That held the chorus to about √x on the far dawns.

And then the echo trap closed: no room for any bird above the middle wire.

In the mathGeometric divisibility estimates → bounds on weighted prime sums → zero-free regions for L-functions, with the exponent ½ + ε for every ε > 0.

The inspector had tugged every knot, using only the oldest, most trusted rules. Within two days, five other inspectors, each working alone, had checked it again and agreed.

“Checked,” said Grey, “is not the same as understood.”

That night Grey told stories, because nobody yet knows why the loom works.

“The very next day,” said Grey, “a weaver named Michael Rubinstein redrew the main road by hand, as one big weighing. Every step is in his own ink but one: an inequality in the middle, which he still copies from the nest.”

In the mathA 164-page draft (21 Nov), written with three AI systems, reads the proof as Step D's two-way count with a better number and reduces it to one inequality, (M). (M) ⇒ RH is written out by hand; (M) itself is still taken from the Lean code.

from the draft's own summary

Grey grew up in a round aviary where counting wraps around like a clock. There, the old question has an answer, and its nest was finished long ago.

Take a curve and count its points on a clock with any number of hours. The count always lands close to the number of hours, and the birds there know exactly how close.

In the mathy² = x³ − x + 1 on a clock with p hours has about p points, never off by more than 2√p: Hasse, 1933; Weil, for every curve. The RH of clock arithmetic, with two classic proofs: Stepanov's balance, and a positivity.

The aviary's proof is short. Grey strung a hammock between two posts. “Its height counts whole things,” said Grey, “so it can never go below the ground.”

Kito tugged it lower and lower. Up to a point it held. Past that point it would have to sink into the earth, and it can't. That point is exactly the limit.

In the mathThe degree of r − s·Frobenius is r² + a·r·s + p·s², a whole number ≥ 0. Never negative ⇒ discriminant ≤ 0 ⇒ a² ≤ 4p ⇒ |a| ≤ 2√p (+9 at p = 41).

“So which is it,” asked Kito, “a balance or a positivity?” “In this loom, both,” said Grey.

“Remember the loom's ‘only so much room’? Here is why. You can't have part of a twig. A strand that isn't empty holds at least one whole twig.” That plain fact is the floor the whole squeeze pushes against.

In the mathStepanov's “roots ≤ degree” has a whole-number twin: a nonzero integer is at least 1 in size. For lattices it is the slope inequality, on the path to the main theorem; Rubinstein's draft draws the same parallel.
