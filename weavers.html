The Weavers and the Middle Wire

A story about the first computer-checked proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.

Press → or tap the arrow to turn the page

Kito hatched in a colony of weaver birds, the kind that tie real knots. Every evening the whole colony settled on three long wires to sing, and every evening somebody asked the old question: will a bird ever perch above the middle wire?

Old Grey, the oldest weaver, always said the same thing. “Ten trillion birds, and not one has. But watching isn't knowing.” In the old tree hung the torn nests of every weaver who had tried to settle it. Kito decided to find out.

Grey began with twigs. “Every bundle in this colony is tied from twigs that can't be split: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11… Twelve is a bundle of 2, 2 and 3, and there's no other way to tie it.”

Kito walked the path looking for them. They lay scattered with no pattern at all: 41 and 43 side by side, then nothing from 89 to 97.

In the mathThe unsplittable twigs are the primes. Every whole number factors into them in exactly one way. Give twig p the length log p, and bundle n has length log n.

At dusk the colony counted its twigs aloud and compared the count with a smooth guess. The two never quite agreed. The difference rose and fell like a murmur: the colony's chorus.

“How loud can the chorus get?” asked Kito. “That,” said Grey, “is the whole question.”

In the mathCount each prime power pk ≤ x with weight log p and subtract x. The Riemann Hypothesis says this error stays about √x in size.

real primes, up to 100,000

Grey tossed a seed, light side up or dark side up, again and again, keeping score. The score wandered, but never far: after n tosses, about √n.

“If the twigs are honest,” said Grey, “the chorus wanders just like my seeds.”

In the mathA fair random walk drifts by about √n. The Riemann Hypothesis says the primes are that fair: no hidden bias, just honest noise.

Grey kept an old songbook, written down by Bernhard Riemann in 1859. Every song in it is made from the twigs.

“Race it two ways,” said Grey. One bird added note after note: 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + ⋯. The other used nothing but twigs, one factor for each, multiplied together. Both stopped at exactly the same mark, π²/6. “So the songbook,” said Grey, “knows the twigs.”

In the mathζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + ⋯ equals a product with one factor per prime (Euler). The zeros of ζ are the birds of this story.

Then Grey showed Kito where the chorus comes from. Hidden on the wires sits a flock of birds, and each one sings a single pure note.

Add their voices and the jagged twig count appears, step by step. With a hundred birds singing it is already close.

In the mathRiemann's explicit formula, proved by von Mangoldt: the prime-count error is a sum of waves, one for each zero of ζ. A zero's position along the line sets its pitch.

The birds perch on three wires. None ever sits outside the outer two, and they come in mirror pairs: a bird above the middle wire would have a twin just as far below.

“Every bird anyone has spotted sits on the middle wire,” said Grey. “More than ten trillion. But the flock never ends.”

In the mathThe zeros lie in the strip 0 < Re s < 1, symmetric about Re s = ½. The Riemann Hypothesis: every one has Re s = ½. Computers have checked more than 1013.

birds at the first 13 zeros

That night Kito couldn't sleep, so Kito flew. The path swung out in long loops and curled back, again and again, while Grey watched from below.

Every so often the flight swept exactly over the home perch. Each time, a bird fluttered down and settled on the middle wire, at the very hour Kito passed overhead. By the end of the night, six birds sat in a row.

In the mathKito's position at hour t is ζ(½ + i·t), and the home perch is ζ = 0. So each pass over it is a zero, ½ + i·t: the first at t = 14.13. These are the birds of the story.

“Why does it matter where they sit?” asked Kito.

Grey hopped up the pole. “On the middle wire a bird sings at the chorus's normal volume. Sit higher, and the voice grows faster. Sooner or later it drowns out everyone.”

In the mathA zero at height β contributes a wave of size xβ. On the middle line that is x½ = √x.

Across the valley there are other colonies. Each paints its twigs with its own repeating pattern of +1 and −1, and each has its own songbook and its own flock.

“The same question hangs over all of them,” said Grey.

In the mathEach pattern is a Dirichlet character with its own L-function. The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis says all their zeros sit on the middle line. This proof covers ζ and every one of them.

One November morning the colony woke to find a new nest in the old tree, among the torn ones. It was finished: 175,187 knots, woven by a mathematician named Levent Alpöge, working with Claude.

Before anyone dared touch it, the knot inspector tugged every knot. Not one slipped. But it was far too big to take in at once. “We won't understand it by staring,” said Grey. “We'll read it like a map.”

In the mathThe proof is 175,187 files of Lean, a language in which a small trusted program, the kernel, checks every logical step.

The map showed six landmarks. “Walk it backwards,” said Grey, “from where we want to end up. And weave a practice nest as we go. That's how a weaver learns one.”

First A, a trap for birds nobody can see. Then B, four piles of twigs. Then D, a balance. Then C, the great loom. E and F lie out toward the Far Ones' country; the main road only leans on them.

In the mathSix steps, A to F, taken in the order in which each answers the question the one before it leaves open.

To hear the chorus clearly, the colony learned to listen to a smoothed version of it, called G.

If every bird sits on the middle wire, G stays inside a thin band, about √x wide, forever. Kito listened all the way out to 200,000. It stayed inside.

In the mathG(x) is a smoothed prime count, Σ Λ(n)(x/n − 1), minus its expected trend. The plot shows G(x)/√x.

real data

“First, a trap,” said Grey, “for birds you can't see. We can't search an endless wire bird by bird. But we can listen: a bird perched too high would make the chorus too loud.

“If it never rises above about √x, even on one side only, no bird can hide above the middle wire. And so its mirror twin can't hide below.” “But how can listening catch anything?” asked Kito. “I'll show you,” said Grey.

In the mathOne-sided bounds are enough: Edmund Landau, 1905.

Grey led Kito to the first landmark, the echo cave. “Call into the dark,” said Grey, “and listen.” A distant bird's echo comes back faint. A near one's comes back stronger with every call, and the nearer the bird, the faster it grows.

A bird above the middle wire would be close enough to make the echoes grow without end.

In the mathThe echoes are the Taylor coefficients of a function built from the chorus. A pole at distance r makes them grow like (1/r)k.

schematic

Then Grey sprang the trap. Echoes that grow without end need a chorus that grows without end. But the chorus has a volume limit. Both can't be true, so no bird sits up there.

“That's all?” said Kito. “That's all,” said Grey, “as long as we can prove the limit.”

In the mathLandau's argument: a zero with Re s > ½ forces a growth that the one-sided bound forbids.

Drag the bird down to the middle wire.

schematic

There was a catch. The weavers can't listen every night. Their method works only on certain stretches of nights, each much longer than the one before, with another always further on. Kito's heart sank.

“Enough,” said Grey. “A high bird is loud everywhere. One dawn far enough out will catch it.” So the task was set: on those dawns, keep the chorus no louder than about √x. That night Kito knotted the first strands of a practice nest to a branch.

In the mathWindows from Y to Y3/2, reaching to infinity, suffice. The bound needed is x½+ε. To get it, the proof ties a bundle, weighs it two ways, and weaves a lattice to settle the difference.

Next on the map: four big piles of twigs, and the first wall. The great loom, further on, is strung from two helper twigs that sort every twig into four piles, and it can weigh only one: the gold pile.

“Why only one?” asked Kito. “Inside the loom,” said Grey, “most twigs split into pieces. Only whole ones can be weighed.”

Kito stared at the other three piles. “Then we're blind to three twigs out of four.”

In the mathThe helpers give each prime two labels, χ, ψ = ±1. Inside the bigger number system some primes split, like 5 = (2 + i)(2 − i), while 3 stays whole. The loom sees the χ = −1 half; averaging narrows it to ψ = +1.

toy helpers 3 and 7four piles, half then one: checked in the Lean codesplit vs. whole: our reading

“Not blind,” said Grey. “Just looking from the wrong side. Write the four piles as four rows of signs, and add the rows.”

Kito did. Every twig outside the gold pile cancelled to nothing; every twig inside counted four times. “So a limit on one pile,” said Kito slowly, “is a limit on all of them?” “If the helpers are quiet,” said Grey. “And we get to choose quiet ones.” Kito wove the standing ring that evening.

In the mathOne pile = ¼ × (all primes + three helper sums): Fourier analysis on four classes. Known averages guarantee good helpers exist.

this exact combination is in the Lean code

The weighing needed help. Past the edge of the colony live the Far Ones, “at infinity”, in directions no bird can point to. From the edge of the colony, Kito saw only a shadow: a cage of light that kept turning itself inside out.

The Far Ones can't count twigs. But they can measure size, and carry strands. They follow fixed rules, and the inspector checks them too.

In the mathBirds stand for the primes. Far Ones stand for the place “at infinity” (size, in the real and complex numbers) and the high-dimensional spaces the proof works in.

At the third landmark stood an old balance, older than anyone in the colony. “Tie the biggest bundle you can. Weigh it by its twigs on this side, and let a Far One weigh it by its size on that one.”

The Far One's shadow turned once, and the scale settled, perfectly level. “So the size,” said Grey, “tells you about the twigs.”

In the mathThe bundle is a! = 1 · 2 · 3 ⋯ a, and Σ (copies of p in a!) · log p = log(a!). Chebyshev, 1850.

“How many 3-twigs are in the big bundle?” asked Grey.

Kito counted in layers. Every third number gives one. Every ninth gives a second. Every 27th, a third, and the last one more. Forty in all. “Layers,” said Grey. “Remember layers.”

In the math81! contains 27 + 9 + 3 + 1 = 40 threes. Legendre's formula: about a/p + a/p² + a/p³ + ⋯ copies of p.

Then the proof added a twist. Grey set out a row of baskets, all the same small size, one for each layer of each twig. Into each basket went that layer's copies, stacked up. Only what stuck up over the rim was kept.

Small twigs stick up a long way. Their higher layers stick up less and less, and far enough along, every stack fits inside its basket.

In the mathWith a = x · b: each basket holds b, and the stack for pe is a/pe tall. Keep (a/pe − b)+; past pe = x it is zero.

Kito weighed the overhangs, each by its twig's length, against what the Far One measured.

The scale tipped, just slightly. And the tilt was exactly the chorus.

That night the practice nest got its roof.

In the mathΣ log p · (a/pe − b)+, minus the size side, equals b · G(x). The trap needs a limit on this gap.

At last they reached the great loom, where the weighing really happens. Kito watched Grey make a single weaving pass. Grey fed out a strand of enormous length, and a Far One carried it over into a partner world, where it was paired with a short one, like thread passed from a big hoop to a small one.

In the mathA pass is a theta lift. The strands are polynomials of degree a and b, with a = x · b (a polynomial's degree is its highest power).

schematic

Kito watched a pass up close. The strand hopped along a row of pegs on the loom, and each peg held a big loop and a small one. At the home peg, the strand's gold, ordinary side went in the big loop.

On the way to the next peg a Far One turned the strand over, through a direction no bird can see. It arrived mirrored, with its gold side in the small loop. At the peg after that it was turned back again.

In the mathEach polynomial has a z part and a z̄ part. At every other peg the two swap roles.

schematicthe turn-over picture: our framing

At each twig there's a magic hop. Each hop shrinks a strand and carries it to the next peg. Every other hop, it lands in a big loop and fits. In between, it must squeeze into a small loop, and whatever sticks out, in red, becomes knots the weave is forced to tie.

“The overhangs again!” said Kito. “The same ones as on the scale.”

In the mathFrobenius, x → xp, divides degree by p, since (u + v)p = up + vp mod p. Odd hops overflow by a/pe − b: Step D's formula.

Grey hopped along the beam, tying a knot of p onto every strand. “Forced knots are good news,” said Grey. “If every strand carries one, the whole weave can be divided by p.”

The mesh grows finer. And to the Far Ones, measuring at infinity, a finer mesh counts as bigger.

In the mathThe polynomials form a lattice. Divisibility at p shrinks its covolume, and this is how the proof builds the lattice it weighs.

Two panes of light hung from the loom. A Far One measured the other pan: the area of a very special lattice of light.

Out of the measurement fell Euler's constant γ, the very number the chorus G subtracts.

In the mathThe Chowla–Selberg formula gives the periods of CM lattices exactly, and γ appears.

Kito worked out one pass and slumped down beside the scale. The books could only be so unbalanced, but the slack was enormous, far bigger than the chorus itself.

“One pass,” said Grey, “tells you nothing.” Kito's first wall was the same: one layer thin, with daylight through it.

In the mathGeometry gives b · G(x) ≤ errors, but the errors are about b · x. Useless on its own.

“Then weave again,” said Grey. Feed one pass into the next, m times over. With each new layer, Kito turned one more bead on the sign cord from minus to plus.

Every pass forces its own knots of p, and the gains add up, layer on layer.

In the mathA chain of m theta lifts. Each turns one minus sign of its group into a plus.

But weaving isn't free. Each new pass opens directions that only the Far Ones can carry, and each pass carries a little more than the one before.

Kito added it up and frowned. The cost grew much faster than the passes did.

In the mathPass j costs about j blocks of new coordinates, so m passes cost about 1 + 2 + ⋯ + m ≈ m²/2.

matches Rubinstein's independent accountthe ½ in m²/2: our arithmetic

Kito wrote it all out as a budget. The prize, the chorus G, is collected once per pass. Against it: an overhead x, paid once, and the weaving's cost.

Share the overhead over m passes, and the chorus must fit inside the rest.

In the mathIn units of b: m · G(x) ≤ x + m², so G(x) ≤ x/m + m. The write-ups call the smaller error lines “budgets” (B₀, Nc); each fits in the same allowance.

"budgets", "allowance": the write-ups' words

How many passes? Too few, and the overhead crushes you. Too many, and the weaving does.

Kito walked down into the valley between, and at the very bottom, where Grey was already waiting, found the answer: the square root of x. And there, the chorus fits exactly where the Riemann Hypothesis needs it. Back home, Kito wove the walls √x layers deep.

In the mathx/m + m is smallest at m = √x, giving G ≤ 2√x.

Grey had seen it nearly fail. In an early count, each pass seemed to re-weave every pass before it, and the cost ran away.

“Then the best you get,” said Grey, “is far too loud, and the trap stays open.” The finished loom pays for each knot exactly once.

In the mathIf the chain cost m³, x/m + m² is smallest near m = x1/3, giving x2/3 instead of √x. Alpöge's timeline: an early count “would stop at 2/3”.

“I've seen this shape before,” said Grey. “Long ago a weaver named Stepanov tied every knot over and over, and with that one trick settled the question in a round aviary far from here.”

“Then why not here?” “To tie a knot twice you need a kind of slope, and whole numbers have none. That wall stood for more than fifty years, until these passes got over it.”

In the mathStepanov, 1969: a polynomial of degree D with roots of multiplicity m at the points gives points × m ≤ D. Here, G × m ≤ x + m². Multiplicity needs derivatives, and integers have none. The author calls each lift “one more order of multiplicity”.

the multiplicity claim is the author's; the comparison is ours

Off the main road, out toward the Far Ones' country, stood the last two landmarks. The Far Ones made sure the loom's many pieces never tangled: in their many directions, almost any two strands run nearly at right angles. And deep inside the giant number systems, the weather stayed calm.

Neither could decide the answer. But without them, the loom would fail. Kito added the doorway last, and wove it tight against the weather.

In the mathE: overlap ≤ about e−xε/8, and it must shrink to nothing. F: Selmer-group noise (cousins of Ш) ≤ exε/2, with room. The ½ comes from Step C.

Now Kito ran the whole story forward. √x passes of the loom put a limit on one pile of twigs. Grey's rows of signs brought back all four piles. That capped the chorus on the far dawns.

And the echo trap left no room for any bird above the middle wire.

In the mathGeometric divisibility estimates → bounds on weighted prime sums → zero-free regions for L-functions, with the exponent ½ + ε for every ε > 0.

The inspector had tugged every knot, using only the oldest, most trusted rules. Within two days, five other inspectors, each working alone, had checked it again and agreed.

“Checked,” said Grey, “is not the same as understood.”

That night Grey told stories, because nobody yet knows why the loom works.

“The very next day,” said Grey, “a weaver named Michael Rubinstein drew the first map by hand. It shows the whole nest as one big weighing, and traces the road from a single knot to the middle wire. The road is drawn in ink. The knot itself is still borrowed from the nest.”

“The blind spot again,” said Kito. “Just like the four piles!”

Grey grew up in a round aviary where counting wraps around like a clock. There, the old question has an answer, and its nest was finished long ago.

Take a curve and count its points on a clock with any number of hours. The count always lands close to the number of hours, and the birds there know exactly how close.

In the mathy² = x³ − x + 1 on a clock with p hours has about p points, never off by more than 2√p: Hasse, 1933; Weil, for every curve. The RH of clock arithmetic, with two classic proofs: Stepanov's balance, and a positivity.

counted live

The aviary's proof is short. Grey strung a hammock between two posts. “Its height counts whole things,” said Grey, “so it can never go below the ground.”

Kito tugged it lower and lower. Up to a point it held. Past that point it would have to sink into the earth, and it can't. That point is exactly the limit.

In the mathThe degree of r − s·Frobenius is r² + a·r·s + p·s², a whole number ≥ 0. Never negative ⇒ discriminant ≤ 0 ⇒ a² ≤ 4p ⇒ |a| ≤ 2√p (+9 at p = 41).

“So which is it,” asked Kito, “a balance or a positivity?”

“In this loom, both,” said Grey. “You can't have part of a twig. A bundle that isn't empty holds at least one whole twig.” That plain fact sits right where the passes are measured.

In the mathStepanov's “roots ≤ degree” has a whole-number twin: a nonzero integer is at least 1 in size. For lattices it is the slope inequality, on the path to the main theorem; Rubinstein's draft draws the same parallel.

Then, on 15 November: wind it through the loom again and again, as many times as the square root. One faint note, echoed through every pass, became a full chorus.

The other two threads are still loose. Does the loom, run in the round aviary, become Stepanov's knot? And why does tying knots over and over make a positivity so strong?
